# Writing and editing

Start from the author's intended claim, audience, supplied facts and requested action. Use approved samples for the same language and genre. Keep distinctive phrasing when it communicates the author's meaning. If samples are absent, use plain language and identify assumptions.

For revisions, preserve the source and show the proposed changes. Protect numbers, denominators, units, dates, names, citations, negation, conditions, uncertainty and causal strength. Flag an unsupported claim with a concrete question or missing source. Add an example or fact only when evidence supports it.

Follow the requested mode. A critique identifies issues, an edit revises supplied prose, and a draft develops the author's supplied points. For a substantial manuscript, establish the claim and supporting evidence before expanding the outline. Give the author a chance to resolve substantive gaps. Review whether each paragraph helps the reader understand or act.

Remove repetitive transitions, vague praise, ornamental metaphors and needless restatement. Preserve technical terms and author intent. In procedures, use consistent terms and one action per step. In Korean formal reports, use nominal endings when the brief requests them. Match email formality to the recipient and approved samples. Identify substantive changes separately from phrasing changes.

Use human review for publication and external correspondence. Keep proposed text as a draft until the user authorizes the external action. Stylistic edits alone do not establish factual accuracy or authorship.
